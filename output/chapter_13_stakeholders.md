# Chapter 13: Valuation for Different Stakeholders

## Valuation for Different Stakeholders

### Opening Vignette: The $100 Million Valuation Dispute

In 2019, a Series B startup faced a crisis: The lead VC valued the company at $100 million pre-money, the founders
insisted on $150 million, the CFO’s 409A valuation came in at $60 million for common stock, and a potential acquirer
offered $120 million. How could the same company have four different valuations?

The answer: Different stakeholders have different perspectives, objectives, and valuation frameworks. VCs focus on
ownership percentage and return multiples. Founders care about dilution and control. The 409A valuation must satisfy IRS
safe harbor requirements. Acquirers evaluate strategic value and synergies. Understanding these different perspectives
is critical for successful fundraising, M&A, and equity compensation.

This chapter explores how different stakeholders approach valuation and how to navigate the inherent conflicts and
trade-offs.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, readers will be able to:

Compare investor perspectives across angels, VCs, corporate VCs, and PE firms, and explain how their return requirements
and time horizons affect valuation

Calculate founder dilution across multiple funding rounds and evaluate trade-offs between valuation and ownership
retention

Analyze acquirer viewpoints distinguishing between strategic and financial buyers and their different valuation
frameworks

Determine appropriate discounts between preferred and common stock for 409A valuations using OPM and PWERM

Evaluate employee equity compensation considering vesting, dilution, and probability-weighted exit scenarios

Assess lender and creditor perspectives on valuation, particularly for venture debt and convertible notes

Navigate conflicts of interest and alignment issues when different stakeholders have competing valuation objectives



### 13.1 Investor Perspectives

Different types of investors have fundamentally different return requirements, risk tolerances, and time horizons,
leading to different valuation frameworks.

#### Angel Investors

Characteristics: - Individual investors using personal capital - Typical investment: $25K-$500K - Target return: 10-30x
(to offset high failure rate) - Time horizon: 5-10 years - Risk tolerance: Very high (expect 70-90% failure rate)

Valuation Approach: Angels often use simple methods (Berkus, Scorecard) and focus on: - Team quality (50% of decision) -
Market size (30%) - Product/traction (20%)

Typical valuations: $2-10M pre-money for seed stage

#### Venture Capital Firms

Characteristics: - Professional investors managing institutional capital - Typical investment: $2M-$50M per round -
Target return: 3-10x (portfolio approach) - Time horizon: 7-10 years (fund life) - Risk tolerance: High (expect 50-70%
failure rate)

Valuation Framework:

VCs use the Venture Capital Method:

$$Post-Money Valuation=\frac{Terminal Value}{Target Return^{Years}}$$

Example: - Projected exit value in 5 years: $500M - Target return: 10x - Post-money valuation: $500M / 10 = $50M -
Investment: $10M - Ownership: 20%

Key Considerations: - Ownership target: 15-25% per round - Anti-dilution protection - Board seats and control rights -
Liquidation preferences

#### Corporate Venture Capital (CVC)

Characteristics: - Corporate investment arms (Google Ventures, Intel Capital, Salesforce Ventures) - Typical investment:
$5M-$50M - Target return: 3-5x (lower than traditional VC) - Time horizon: Flexible (no fund expiration) - Strategic
objectives: Market intelligence, partnerships, potential acquisition

Valuation Approach:

CVCs often pay 20-40% premiums over financial VCs because: - Strategic value beyond financial return - Competitive
dynamics (do not want competitors to invest) - Longer time horizons - Access to corporate resources

Example: - Financial VC values company at $100M - CVC values strategic partnership at $20M - CVC willing to pay $120M
(20% premium)

#### Private Equity Firms

Characteristics: - Focus on later-stage, growth equity, or buyouts - Typical investment: $25M-$500M+ - Target return:
2-3x (lower risk than VC) - Time horizon: 4-7 years - Risk tolerance: Lower (invest in profitable or near-profitable
companies)

Valuation Framework:

PE firms use traditional DCF and comparable company analysis:

$$Valuation=EBITDA×Multiple$$

Typical multiples: 8-15x EBITDA for growth companies

Key Considerations: - Path to profitability - Scalability of business model - Exit options (IPO, strategic sale,
secondary sale) - Management team capability



### 13.2 Founder Considerations

Founders must balance valuation maximization with dilution management, control retention, and long-term value creation.

#### Dilution Analysis

Single Round Dilution:

$$Founder Ownership After Round=Ownership Before×(1-\frac{Investment}{Post-Money Valuation})$$

Example: - Founders own 100% before funding - Raise $5M at $20M post-money - Dilution: $5M / $20M = 25% - Founder
ownership after: 75%

Multi-Round Dilution:

Dilution compounds across rounds:

After four rounds, founders own only 36% despite the company growing from $5M to $190M valuation.

#### Valuation vs. Terms Trade-Off

Higher valuation does not always mean better outcome for founders:

Scenario A: High Valuation, Tough Terms - Valuation: $100M - Investment: $20M - Ownership: 20% - Terms: 2x liquidation
preference, full ratchet anti-dilution - Exit at $150M: Investors get $40M (2x preference), founders get $110M × 80% =
$88M

Scenario B: Lower Valuation, Founder-Friendly Terms - Valuation: $80M - Investment: $20M - Ownership: 25% - Terms: 1x
liquidation preference, weighted average anti-dilution - Exit at $150M: Investors get $37.5M (25%), founders get $112.5M
(75%)

Scenario B delivers more value to founders despite lower valuation.

#### Control Considerations

Founders should consider: - Board composition (founder seats, investor seats, independent seats) - Voting rights
(protective provisions, super-majority requirements) - Drag-along rights (can investors force a sale?) - Right of first
refusal (can founders sell shares?)

Rule of Thumb: Maintain >50% voting control through Series A if possible, >33% through Series B (blocking minority).

### 13.3 Acquirer Viewpoints

Acquirers value companies differently than investors, focusing on strategic fit, synergies, and integration costs.

#### Strategic vs. Financial Buyers

Strategic Buyers (Corporations): - Value synergies: Revenue synergies (cross-sell, market access) and cost synergies
(eliminate redundancies) - Willing to pay 30-100% premiums over standalone value - Focus on strategic fit, cultural
alignment, integration ease

Financial Buyers (PE Firms): - Value standalone cash flows - Focus on operational improvements, multiple arbitrage -
Typically pay 10-30% premiums over market value

#### Synergy Valuation

$$Acquisition Value=Standalone Value+PV(Synergies)-Integration Costs$$

Example: - Target standalone value: $100M - Revenue synergies: $20M NPV (cross-selling to acquirer’s customer base) -
Cost synergies: $15M NPV (eliminate duplicate functions) - Integration costs: $10M - Acquisition value: $100M + $20M +
$15M - $10M = $125M

Synergy Realization Probability: - Revenue synergies: 30-50% realized (difficult to achieve) - Cost synergies: 70-90%
realized (easier to achieve)

Risk-Adjusted Synergy Value: - Revenue synergies: $20M × 40% = $8M - Cost synergies: $15M × 80% = $12M - Total
risk-adjusted synergies: $20M

Risk-Adjusted Acquisition Value: $100M + $20M - $10M = $110M



### 13.4 409A Valuations and Common Stock Discounts

IRS Section 409A requires private companies to obtain independent valuations of common stock for option grants. These
valuations are typically 40-90% lower than preferred stock valuations.

#### Preferred vs. Common Stock

Why Common Stock is Worth Less:

Liquidation Preferences: Preferred stockholders get paid first in exit

No Voting Rights: Common stock often has limited or no voting rights

No Dividends: Preferred may have dividend rights

Conversion Rights: Preferred can convert to common, but not vice versa

Anti-Dilution Protection: Preferred has downside protection

#### Option Pricing Method (OPM)

Treats common stock as a call option on enterprise value:

$$Common Stock Value=Call Option Value(Strike=Liquidation Preference)$$

Using Black-Scholes:

$$C=V×N(d_{1})-K×e^{-rT}×N(d_{2})$$

where: -  = Current enterprise value -  = Liquidation preference (strike price) -  = Time to exit -  = Volatility

Example: - Enterprise value: $100M - Preferred liquidation preference: $40M - Time to exit: 3 years - Volatility: 60% -
Risk-free rate: 4%

Using Black-Scholes: Common stock value = $65M

$$
Discount: ($100M - $65M) / $100M = 35%
$$

#### Probability-Weighted Expected Return Method (PWERM)

Models different exit scenarios and calculates expected value:

$$
Expected Common Value: 0.20($92M) + 0.50($55M) + 0.20($4M) + 0.10($0) = $46.7M
$$

If preferred value is $100M, common discount is 53%.

### 13.5 Employee Equity Valuation

Employees receiving stock options must understand the true value considering vesting, dilution, and exit probability.

#### Option Value Components

Intrinsic Value:

$$Intrinsic Value=max(0,Current FMV-Strike Price)$$

Time Value:

Options have time value until expiration (typically 10 years, but only 90 days post-termination).

Total Value:

$$Option Value=Intrinsic Value+Time Value$$

#### Probability-Weighted Employee Value

Employees should consider:

$$
Expected Value: 0.20($1.6M) + 0.30($320K) + 0.20($40K) + 0.30($0) = $424K
$$

Vesting Adjustment:

If 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff: - Year 1: 0% vested - Year 2: 25% vested - Year 3: 50% vested - Year 4: 100% vested

Expected value in Year 1: $0 (not yet vested) Expected value in Year 2: $424K × 25% = $106K Expected value in Year 4:
$424K

#### Cash vs. Equity Compensation

Employees should compare:

Cash Compensation: - Certain (received regardless of company performance) - Liquid (can spend immediately) - Taxed as
ordinary income (up to 37%)

Equity Compensation: - Uncertain (depends on exit) - Illiquid (4-year vesting, 7-10 year exit) - Taxed as capital gains
(20%) if held >1 year

Break-Even Analysis:

If offered $150K salary + $100K options vs. $180K salary:

$$
Expected option value: $100K \times 20% (exit probability) = $20K After-tax: $20K \times 80% = $16K Equivalent cash:
$16K / (1 - 0.37) = $25K
$$

| Scenario | Probability | Exit Value | Option Value | After-Tax Value |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Successful Exit | 20% | $500M | $2M | $1.6M (20% tax) |
| Moderate Exit | 30% | $100M | $400K | $320K |
| Small Exit | 20% | $30M | $50K | $40K |
| Failure | 30% | $0 | $0 | $0 |

The equity package is worth $150K + $25K = $175K equivalent, less than $180K all-cash.



### 13.6 Lender and Creditor Perspectives

Lenders (venture debt, banks) and creditors value companies focusing on downside protection and asset coverage.

#### Venture Debt Valuation

Venture debt providers lend to startups, typically: - Loan amount: 20-40% of last equity round - Interest rate: 8-12% -
Warrants: 5-15% warrant coverage

Valuation Considerations: - Runway extension (how many months of cash does debt provide?) - Dilution avoidance (debt is
less dilutive than equity) - Downside protection (senior to equity in liquidation)

Example: - Company raises $10M Series A at $40M post-money - Takes $3M venture debt (30% of equity round) - Warrants:
10% coverage = $300K / $40M × 100% = 0.75% equity

Dilution comparison: - Equity: $3M / $40M = 7.5% dilution - Debt: 0.75% dilution (warrants only) - Dilution savings:
6.75%

But debt must be repaid (unlike equity), creating cash flow burden.

#### Asset-Based Valuation

Lenders focus on liquidation value:

$$Liquidation Value=∑(Asset Value×Recovery Rate)$$

Typical recovery rates: - Cash: 100% - Accounts receivable: 70-90% - Inventory: 30-60% - Equipment: 20-40% -
IP/Goodwill: 0-10%

Example: - Cash: $5M × 100% = $5M - AR: $3M × 80% = $2.4M - Equipment: $2M × 30% = $600K - Liquidation value: $8M

Lenders typically lend 50-70% of liquidation value, so maximum loan: $4-5.6M.


### Key Takeaways

Different investors have different return requirements: Angels target 10-30x, VCs target 3-10x, CVCs accept 3-5x due to
strategic value, PE firms target 2-3x with lower risk

Founder dilution compounds across rounds: Raising four rounds (seed through Series C) typically results in founders
owning only 30-40% despite company value increasing 40-50x

Valuation and terms must be evaluated together: A lower valuation with 1x liquidation preference can deliver more
founder value than higher valuation with 2x preference and full ratchet anti-dilution

Strategic acquirers pay 30-100% premiums over standalone value for synergies, but only 30-50% of revenue synergies are
typically realized vs. 70-90% of cost synergies

Common stock is worth 30-80% less than preferred stock depending on stage, with discounts calculated using OPM or PWERM
for 409A compliance

Employee stock options have expected value of only 10-30% of nominal value after adjusting for exit probability,
vesting, dilution, and taxes

Lenders focus on downside protection and liquidation value, typically lending 50-70% of asset-based liquidation value,
making venture debt less dilutive but creating cash flow obligations
