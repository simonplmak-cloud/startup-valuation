Valuing pre-revenue and pre-profit companies is not only a financial exercise but also a regulatory requirement. This
chapter provides a summary of the key regulatory guidelines that govern fair value measurement, including IFRS 13, ASC
820, and IRS 409A. It also includes a compliance checklist to help ensure that valuation reports meet the required
standards.

### 7.1: IFRS 13 and ASC 820 Fair Value Principles

IFRS 13 and ASC 820 are the international and U.S. accounting standards for fair value measurement, respectively. They
are largely converged and provide a common framework for measuring fair value.

Definition of Fair Value

Fair value is defined as the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly
transaction between market participants at the measurement date. This definition emphasizes several key concepts. First,
fair value is an exit price, not an entry price. Second, the transaction is assumed to be orderly, meaning that it is
not a forced or distressed sale. Third, the transaction is between market participants, meaning that both parties are
knowledgeable, willing, and able to transact.

The Fair Value Hierarchy

Both IFRS 13 and ASC 820 establish a three-level hierarchy for the inputs used in valuation techniques. The hierarchy
prioritizes observable inputs over unobservable inputs:

Level 1: Quoted prices in active markets for identical assets or liabilities. This is the most reliable form of evidence
and should be used whenever available.

Level 2: Inputs other than quoted prices that are observable, either directly or indirectly. Examples include quoted
prices for similar assets, interest rates, and yield curves.

Level 3: Unobservable inputs. These are inputs that are not based on observable market data and must be developed using
the entity’s own assumptions. Level 3 measurements require the most judgment and are subject to the greatest
uncertainty.

Valuation Techniques

The standards permit the use of multiple valuation techniques, including:

Market Approach: Uses prices and other relevant information from market transactions involving identical or comparable
assets or liabilities. Examples include the use of valuation multiples.

Income Approach: Converts future amounts (e.g., cash flows or earnings) to a single present value. Examples include
discounted cash flow analysis and option pricing models.

Cost Approach: Reflects the amount that would be required to replace the service capacity of an asset (often referred to
as current replacement cost).

The choice of technique should be appropriate for the circumstances and should maximize the use of relevant observable
inputs.

Disclosure Requirements

For Level 3 measurements, extensive disclosures are required, including:

A description of the valuation technique(s) used.

The inputs used in the valuation and how they were determined.

A quantitative sensitivity analysis showing the effect of changes in unobservable inputs on the fair value measurement.

A narrative description of the sensitivity of the fair value to changes in unobservable inputs.

### 7.2: IRS Business Valuation Guidelines and 409A Valuation Requirements

In the United States, the IRS has its own set of business valuation guidelines, which are outlined in Revenue Ruling
59-60. These guidelines are used for estate and gift tax purposes and are also influential in other valuation contexts.

Revenue Ruling 59-60

Revenue Ruling 59-60 identifies eight factors to consider when valuing a closely held business:

The nature and history of the business.

The economic outlook in general and the condition and outlook of the specific industry in particular.

The book value of the stock and the financial condition of the business.

The earning capacity of the company.

The dividend-paying capacity.

Whether or not the enterprise has goodwill or other intangible value.

Sales of the stock and the size of the block of stock to be valued.

The market price of stocks of corporations engaged in the same or a similar line of business having their stocks
actively traded in a free and open market.

IRS Section 409A

Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code governs the taxation of nonqualified deferred compensation, which includes
stock options. Under 409A, stock options must be granted with an exercise price that is at least equal to the fair
market value (FMV) of the underlying stock on the date of grant. Failure to comply with 409A can result in significant
tax penalties for the employee, including immediate taxation of the deferred compensation, a 20% additional tax, and
interest charges.

To comply with 409A, companies must determine the FMV of their common stock using a reasonable valuation method. The IRS
provides a “safe harbor” for valuations that meet certain criteria:

Independent Appraisal Safe Harbor: The valuation is performed by a qualified independent appraiser. The appraiser must
have significant knowledge and experience in performing similar valuations. The valuation must be based on a reasonable
application of a reasonable valuation method.

Formula Safe Harbor: The valuation is based on a formula specified in the company’s organizational documents or by the
board of directors.

Illiquid Startup Safe Harbor: For illiquid stock of a startup, the valuation is performed by someone with significant
knowledge and experience in performing similar valuations, and the valuation is evidenced by a written report.

A valuation is presumed to be reasonable if it is less than 12 months old and no material events have occurred since the
valuation date. If the IRS challenges a valuation, the burden of proof is on the IRS to show that the valuation is
unreasonable, provided the company has obtained a safe harbor valuation.

📊 Real World Example: WeWork’s Valuation Collapse and Regulatory Scrutiny (2019)

WeWork’s failed IPO in September 2019 triggered intense regulatory scrutiny of private company valuations. The company’s
valuation plummeted from $47 billion (January 2019 funding round) to $8 billion (October 2019 SoftBank rescue) in just
nine months, raising questions about valuation practices.

The Valuation Challenge: - January 2019: SoftBank invested at $47B valuation - August 2019: IPO filing revealed massive
losses ($1.9B on $1.8B revenue) - September 2019: IPO withdrawn, CEO ousted - October 2019: SoftBank rescue at $8B
valuation (83% decline) - SEC investigation into disclosure practices

Regulatory Issues: 1. IFRS 13 / ASC 820 Compliance: - Was the $47B valuation based on Level 3 inputs properly
documented? - Did WeWork’s financial statements reflect fair value? - Were sensitivity analyses performed and disclosed?

409A Valuation Problems:

Common stock valued at fraction of preferred for option grants

proper Level 3 documentation and risk assessment.

### Key Takeaways


Most startup valuations are Level 3 under IFRS 13/ASC 820, requiring extensive documentation of unobservable inputs,
assumptions, and sensitivity analysis

409A safe harbor requires independent appraisal: Using a qualified independent appraiser shifts the burden of proof to
the IRS in case of challenge

Preferred vs. common stock discounts are defensible but must be well-documented using OPM, PWERM, or other accepted
methods--discounts of 50-90% are common

Documentation is your defense: In audits and litigation, the quality of documentation matters more than the specific
valuation number

Update frequency matters: Material events (new funding, product launch, market changes) require updated valuations for
409A compliance

Disclosure requirements are extensive: Level 3 measurements require disclosure of valuation techniques, inputs,
sensitivity analysis, and reconciliation of changes

Regulatory scrutiny is increasing: Post-WeWork, auditors and regulators are more skeptical of “unicorn” valuations and
demand greater rigor and independence
