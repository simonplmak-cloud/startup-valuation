Valuation is not just a matter of finance and accounting; it is also a legal concept that is frequently at the center of
corporate and commercial disputes. This chapter examines how courts have approached the valuation of pre-revenue and
pre-profit companies, with a particular focus on the legal precedents that have been set in key jurisdictions such as
the Delaware Court of Chancery.

### 8.1: Valuation Disputes in Litigation

Valuation disputes can arise in a variety of legal contexts. Understanding these contexts is important because the legal
standard for valuation may differ depending on the type of dispute.

Shareholder Oppression

Minority shareholders may claim that the majority shareholders have acted oppressively by, for example, forcing a sale
of the company at a price that is below its fair value, diluting their ownership through unfair financing rounds, or
denying them access to information. In these cases, the court may be asked to determine the fair value of the minority
shareholders’ stake.

Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A)

In many jurisdictions, shareholders who dissent from a merger or acquisition have the right to seek an appraisal of
their shares by a court. This is known as appraisal rights or dissenters’ rights. The court will determine the fair
value of the shares immediately before the merger, excluding any value arising from the merger itself.

Tax Disputes

The tax authorities may challenge a company’s valuation for the purposes of assessing taxes on stock-based compensation,
estate and gift transfers, or transfer pricing. The company must defend its valuation using acceptable methods and
supporting documentation.

Divorce Proceedings

In divorce cases, the valuation of a business owned by one or both spouses may be a key issue in the division of marital
assets. The court will determine the fair market value of the business as of the date of separation or another relevant
date.

### 8.2: Analysis of Hyde Park v. FairXchange

The case of Hyde Park Venture Partners v. FairXchange, LLC (Del. Ch. 2024) provides a recent and insightful example of
how the Delaware Court of Chancery approaches the valuation of an early-stage company.

Background

Hyde Park Venture Partners was an investor in FairXchange, a venture-backed financial technology company. When
FairXchange was acquired, Hyde Park exercised its appraisal rights, arguing that the company should have been sold for
hundreds of millions of dollars more than the actual deal price. Hyde Park presented a discounted cash flow (DCF)
analysis to support its valuation.

Court’s Decision

The court ultimately concluded that the deal price in the acquisition was the most reliable indicator of the company’s
fair value. The court called the deal price the “least bad” methodology, acknowledging that valuing an early-stage
company is inherently difficult and that all valuation methods have limitations.

The court gave little weight to the DCF analysis presented by Hyde Park. The court noted that the projections used in
the DCF were highly speculative, given the company’s limited operating history and uncertain future. The court also
noted that the company’s own 409A valuation, which was performed closer to the acquisition date, supported a valuation
much closer to the deal price than to Hyde Park’s DCF valuation.

Significance

The Hyde Park decision is significant for several reasons. First, it highlights the Delaware court’s preference for
market-based evidence of value, such as the deal price in an arm’s-length transaction. Second, it demonstrates the
court’s skepticism of DCF valuations for early-stage companies with limited financial history. Third, it underscores the
importance of having contemporaneous valuations (such as 409A valuations) that can corroborate the deal price.

The decision also serves as a cautionary tale for investors who might challenge a deal price in an appraisal proceeding.
Unless there is clear evidence that the deal price was the result of a flawed process or conflicts of interest, courts
are likely to defer to the market evidence.

### 8.3: How Courts Evaluate Expert Testimony

Courts do not simply accept the conclusions of expert witnesses at face value. They carefully scrutinize the
methodologies and assumptions used by the experts to ensure that they are reliable and well-supported. The factors that
courts consider when evaluating expert testimony on valuation include:

The Expert’s Qualifications and Experience

The court will examine the expert’s education, professional credentials, and experience in performing valuations. An
expert with a strong track record and relevant experience is more likely to be found credible.

The Reliability of the Data Used

The court will assess the quality and reliability of the data used by the expert. Data from reputable sources, such as
public company filings or industry databases, is more reliable than data from unverified or biased sources.

The Appropriateness of the Valuation Methodology

The court will evaluate whether the valuation methodology used by the expert is appropriate for the circumstances. For
example, a DCF analysis may not be appropriate for a pre-revenue company with highly uncertain cash flows.

The Reasonableness of the Assumptions

The court will scrutinize the assumptions made by the expert, such as growth rates, discount rates, and exit multiples.
Assumptions that are not supported by evidence or that are inconsistent with industry norms are likely to be rejected.

The Expert’s Independence and Objectivity

The court will consider whether the expert is independent and objective or whether the expert’s opinion has been
influenced by the party that hired them. An expert who has a financial interest in the outcome of the case or who has a
history of providing biased opinions is less likely to be found credible.

### 8.4: Disputes Involving SAFEs and Convertible Notes

Simple Agreements for Future Equity (SAFEs) and convertible notes are popular financing instruments for early-stage
startups. However, they can also create valuation challenges and disputes.

Valuation Challenges

SAFEs and convertible notes are not straight equity. They are hybrid instruments that convert into equity at a later
date, typically upon a subsequent financing round or an exit event. The conversion terms can be complex and may include
valuation caps, discount rates, and other features. This complexity makes it difficult to value the company on a fully
diluted basis.

Disputes

Disputes can arise between investors and the company over the interpretation of the conversion terms, the valuation cap,
or the discount rate. For example, if the company raises a subsequent financing round at a valuation that is below the
valuation cap, the SAFE or convertible note holders may receive a larger ownership stake than the company anticipated.
This can lead to disputes over dilution and control.

Best Practices

To avoid disputes, it is important to clearly document the terms of SAFEs and convertible notes and to perform a
valuation analysis that takes into account the potential dilutive effect of these instruments. The valuation should be
performed on a fully diluted basis, assuming the conversion of all outstanding SAFEs and convertible notes into equity.


📊 Real World Example: Dell Appraisal Litigation - Court Rejected Deal Price (2016)

In In re Appraisal of Dell Inc., the Delaware Chancery Court rejected the $24.9 billion buyout price negotiated between
Michael Dell and Silver Lake, instead valuing the company at $28.6 billion using DCF analysis. This case shocked the M&A
community by suggesting deal price might not be the best evidence of fair value.

The Case: - 2013: Michael Dell and Silver Lake took Dell private at $13.75 per share ($24.9B total) - Deal process:
45-day go-shop period, special committee, multiple bidders - Dissenting shareholders filed appraisal action seeking
higher valuation - Trial: 11 days of testimony, dueling expert witnesses

Deal Price | N/A | $13.75 | Rejected | | DCF | $28.61 | $12.68 | $17.62 | | Comparable Companies | Not used | Not used |
Not used | | Final Value | $28.61 | $13.75 | $17.62 |

| Factor | Value |
| :--- | :---: |
| Sound Idea | $500,000 |
| Prototype | $500,000 |
| Quality Management Team | $500,000 |
| Strategic Relationships | $0 |
| Product Rollout or Sales | $0 |
| Total Valuation | $1,500,000 |

Court’s Reasoning: 1. Deal price not reliable: Management conflicts of interest (Michael Dell was buyer) 2. Go-shop was
ineffective: 45 days insufficient for complex deal 3. DCF more reliable: Based on management’s own projections 4.
Adjusted for synergies: Added back value of going-private benefits

Key Findings: - Court increased value by 28% above deal price - Awarded $6 billion to dissenting shareholders -
Established that deal price is not automatically the best evidence

Outcome: - 2017: Delaware Supreme Court reversed, holding deal price was best evidence - Rationale: Robust market
process with multiple bidders - But: Left open possibility of rejecting deal price in cases with conflicts

Lesson: Even arms-length deal prices can be challenged in court if there are conflicts of interest or process
deficiencies. Courts will perform independent DCF analysis if they find the deal process unreliable. For startup
valuations, this means: (1) document the deal process thoroughly, (2) ensure independence of valuation, (3) be prepared
to defend DCF assumptions in litigation.

Source: In re Appraisal of Dell Inc., 2016 WL 3186538 (Del. Ch. May 31, 2016), reversed, 177 A.3d 1 (Del. 2017)


### Key Takeaways


Courts prefer market-based evidence (deal prices, recent funding rounds) over complex financial models, but will reject
market evidence if process was flawed

Hyde Park v. FairXchange established that for early-stage companies, recent financing round prices are the “least bad”
methodology despite imperfections

Delaware Supreme Court’s DFC Global decision created a presumption that deal price is best evidence if the sale process
was robust and free from conflicts

Expert witness credibility matters: Courts scrutinize unrealistic assumptions, cherry-picked comparables, and
advocacy-driven analysis

Documentation is critical for litigation: Courts expect to see sensitivity analysis, consideration of alternative
methods, and transparent assumptions

Burden of proof varies by context: In appraisal proceedings, both sides bear burden; in tax cases, taxpayer must prove
IRS wrong; in fraud cases, plaintiff bears burden

Legal precedents evolve: The pendulum has swung from favoring DCF (Dell 2016) to favoring deal price (Dell 2017
reversal, DFC Global) and back, so practitioners must stay current
